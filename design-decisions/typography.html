Design Decisions: Typography

This document records the research-backed reasoning behind the font and typographic choices on ampmanufacturing.com.

1. Body & Display Font: Baskerville

The Errol Morris / New York Times Experiment (2012)

The single largest study on font and believability ever conducted.

  • Sample size: 45,000+ respondents
  • Researchers: Errol Morris (filmmaker/NYT contributor), David Dunning (Cornell psychology professor)
  • Method: Readers took what appeared to be a quiz on the NYT website. They read a passage and indicated whether they agreed. The passage was randomly displayed in one of six typefaces. Readers had no idea the font was the variable being tested.

Fonts tested: Baskerville, Computer Modern, Georgia, Helvetica, Comic Sans, Trebuchet

FontWeighted Agreement Score
Baskerville0.885
Computer Modern0.853
Helvetica0.767
Trebuchet0.753
Georgia0.724
Comic Sans0.715

Result: Baskerville had the highest rate of agreement AND the lowest rate of disagreement. The p-value was 0.0068, confirming statistical significance. Dunning called the effect "rather large" given the uncontrolled online environment: "It is clear in the data that Baskerville is different from the other fonts."

Why it works: Baskerville is a 250-year-old transitional serif designed by John Baskerville in 1757. Its sharp, high-contrast letterforms carry associations with established printing, academia, and historical gravitas without feeling decorative or attention-seeking.

Supporting research:
  • Monotype/Neurons neuroscience study (2022-2023): 400+ participants across 8 countries. Serif typefaces scored +9% on trustworthiness.
  • Piovesan et al. meta-analysis (2023): Reviewed 34 studies covering 635 typefaces. Serif typefaces were consistently associated with formality, tradition, stability, and authority.
  • Song & Schwarz (Yale/Michigan, 2008-2010): Processing fluency research. Statements in clear, high-contrast fonts are judged as more true. "If it's easy to read, it's easy to do, pretty, good, and true."
Our decision: Both body and display fonts are set to Libre Baskerville (Google Fonts web version of Baskerville). This is the single most empirically validated choice for maximizing reader belief and trust.

2. Lowercase Tagline: "the core of what we build is trust"

The premium brand pattern

All-lowercase typography is a deliberate design choice used by premium and luxury brands to signal confidence, sophistication, and understatement. The logic: when you're confident in what you are, you don't need to shout.

  • Apple uses lowercase across product pages, taglines, and marketing copy.
  • Aesop (luxury skincare) uses all-lowercase for product names and descriptions.
  • Glossier, Mejuri, Everlane and other premium DTC brands follow the same pattern.

Uppercase signals authority through volume. Lowercase signals authority through restraint. For a manufacturing company that wants to project quiet confidence and craftsmanship rather than aggressive salesmanship, lowercase is the more credible register.

Our decision: The hero tagline uses CSS text-transform: lowercase to render "the core of what we build is trust" in all lowercase. This aligns with the understated, premium positioning of the brand.
