Our Story

How we got here

We had a contact through our social network who'd been in the inductor business for 40 years. He needed parts built and was willing to show us how the industry worked. What kind of equipment and facility we'd need, for example, and what buyers of inductors expect.

His advice had limits, however, and we were on our own when we decided to set up a winding facility in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. We chose Ho Chi Minh City because of its ties to U.S. commerce and its deep labor pool of educated technicians and engineers.

It took us about two years, a lot of wrong turns, and a few expensive lessons to bring a top level team together across time zones. Our winding lead runs the production floor with surgical precision. Our QC team flags units before we even see the data. When we call the facility at midnight California time, someone picks up. Problems get surfaced immediately, and everyone shares in the wins. Legally registered in Vietnam and the United States, we went looking for our first client.

Our first client was NK Technologies in San Jose, a company specializing in current sensors for industrial power monitoring. We approached them cold and got a lunch meeting with their buyer. She liked us enough to bring her CTO to a second lunch. We sat down across the table from two experienced manufacturing executives and told them the truth: one of us is a university professor, the other a psychotherapist, and we teamed up to build an inductor company from scratch.

They laughed. Then they gave us their drawings.

With precision turn counts, DCR tolerances, and lead lengths down to the hundredth of an inch, we delivered the samples. Two days later, they passed every test in NK's lab. NK placed their first production order with us shortly after. We've been supplying them since.

One thing most people don't know about this business is that the hardest part, in the beginning, is shipping. Shipping 50,000 copper-wound inductors at once involves coordinating international freight, lining up forwarders (good ones, most aren't), figuring out customs timing, and (how could we forget) efficiently handling tariff law at ports of entry.

Today we review the test data on every batch personally. We video call our winding floor during production runs. Every shipment leaves with our signatures on it, and we exchange personal Christmas cards with our forwarders. That's how two outsiders created Ampersand Manufacturing.
