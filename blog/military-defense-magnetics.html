Military and Defense Applications of Custom Magnetic Components

Defense electronics operate in the harshest environments on earth. The magnetic components inside them must meet standards that go far beyond commercial requirements.

Why Defense Is Different

Military electronic systems operate in conditions that would destroy most commercial components. Temperatures range from minus 55 degrees Celsius in high-altitude aircraft to plus 125 degrees Celsius (or higher) near engine compartments and power electronics. Vibration levels on aircraft, vehicles, and shipboard equipment subject every component to sustained mechanical stress. Humidity, salt spray, sand, dust, altitude, and rapid pressure changes are routine operating conditions.

The magnetic components in these systems, the inductors, transformers, and chokes, must survive all of this while maintaining their electrical parameters within specification. A power supply inductor that drifts out of tolerance at temperature can cause the entire system to malfunction. A transformer with a cracked core from vibration can fail catastrophically. The stakes are high: these systems protect lives.

MIL-Spec Standards

The U.S. Department of Defense maintains a comprehensive set of standards that govern the design, manufacture, and testing of components used in military equipment. For magnetic components, the most relevant standards include:

Standard Title Scope
MIL-PRF-27 Transformers and Inductors (Audio, Power, and Pulse) Performance requirements for military-grade transformers and inductors
MIL-STD-981 Design, Manufacturing, and Quality Standards for Custom Electromagnetic Devices Manufacturing process controls and workmanship
MIL-STD-202 Test Methods for Electronic and Electrical Component Parts Environmental and mechanical test procedures
MIL-STD-810 Environmental Engineering Considerations and Laboratory Tests Environmental testing methods (temperature, vibration, shock, humidity)
MIL-I-46058 Insulating Compound, Electrical (for Conformal Coating) Conformal coating materials and application

MIL-PRF-27 is the primary specification for military transformers and inductors. It defines performance grades based on operating temperature range, establishes qualification testing requirements, and specifies the documentation that must accompany each lot of components. Compliance with this standard is typically a non-negotiable requirement for components going into defense platforms.

Extended Temperature Ranges

Commercial electronic components are typically rated for 0 to 70 degrees Celsius (the "commercial" temperature range) or minus 40 to plus 85 degrees Celsius (the "industrial" range). Military applications demand much wider ranges.

Temperature Grade Range Typical Application
Commercial 0 to +70 C Indoor, climate-controlled environments
Industrial -40 to +85 C Outdoor, factory floor, vehicles
Military (Class S) -55 to +125 C Avionics, shipboard, ground vehicles
Military (Class H) -55 to +155 C Near-engine, high-power systems

Meeting these temperature requirements affects every material choice in the component. The core material must maintain its magnetic properties across the full range. The wire insulation must remain intact and flexible at both extremes. The coating and adhesives must withstand thermal cycling without cracking, delaminating, or outgassing. Solder joints must survive the repeated expansion and contraction of thermal cycling over thousands of cycles.

Material Selection for Temperature

Standard enamel wire insulation rated to 155 degrees Celsius (Class F) works for most military applications. For Class H temperature requirements, polyimide-insulated wire (rated to 200 degrees Celsius or higher) is specified. Core coatings shift from standard epoxy to high-temperature formulations or silicone-based systems.

Vibration and Shock Resistance

Magnetic components on military platforms must survive vibration profiles that are orders of magnitude more severe than commercial environments. A transformer mounted in an aircraft experiences continuous vibration from engines, aerodynamic buffeting, and structural resonances throughout every flight. Ground vehicles encounter road-induced vibration that spans a wide frequency range. Shipboard equipment faces hull vibration from engines and wave impact.

Design Considerations

Several design techniques improve a magnetic component's ability to survive vibration and mechanical shock.

  • Vacuum impregnation: The wound component is placed in a vacuum chamber, and liquid varnish or epoxy is drawn into every gap between turns. When cured, the winding becomes a solid mass with no room for individual wires to shift or chafe against each other.
  • Potting: The entire assembly is encapsulated in a rigid compound (typically epoxy or silicone) inside a housing. This eliminates all internal movement and provides excellent resistance to shock and vibration.
  • Lead strain relief: Wire leads exiting the component are the most vulnerable point during vibration. Proper strain relief, through adhesive anchoring, sleeving, or mechanical clamping, prevents lead fatigue and breakage.
  • Core bonding: Multi-piece core assemblies (such as E-I cores or U-cores) are bonded or banded together to prevent the pieces from separating under shock loads.

Testing Standards

Vibration and shock testing follows the procedures defined in MIL-STD-202 and MIL-STD-810. Typical test profiles include sinusoidal vibration sweeps across a frequency range (commonly 10 Hz to 2,000 Hz), random vibration at specified power spectral density levels, and mechanical shock pulses of 50g to 500g depending on the application class.

ITAR Considerations

The International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR) govern the export of defense-related articles, services, and technical data. Components manufactured for defense platforms may fall under ITAR control, which imposes specific requirements on the manufacturer.

  • Facility registration: Manufacturers handling ITAR-controlled articles must register with the Directorate of Defense Trade Controls (DDTC).
  • Technology control: Technical data related to ITAR-controlled components (drawings, specifications, test data) must be protected from access by non-U.S. persons unless properly authorized.
  • Personnel screening: Employees with access to ITAR-controlled information must be U.S. persons (citizens, permanent residents, or protected individuals).
  • Record keeping: Detailed records of all ITAR-controlled articles and technical data must be maintained for a minimum of five years.
ITAR Compliance

ITAR compliance is a facility-level requirement. Buyers sourcing components for defense programs should verify their supplier's ITAR registration status early in the procurement process. Working with a non-registered supplier on ITAR-controlled items creates compliance exposure for both parties.

Documentation and Traceability

Military procurement requires documentation far beyond what commercial customers typically expect. Every shipment of military-grade magnetic components should include:

  • Certificate of Compliance (C of C): Certifies that the components were manufactured in accordance with the applicable specification and drawing revision.
  • Test data: Electrical test results for each unit (DCR, inductance, hi-pot), traceable to calibrated test equipment with current calibration certificates.
  • Material certifications: Mill certifications for core materials, wire certifications showing gauge, insulation type, and thermal class. Solder certifications confirming alloy composition.
  • Lot traceability: The ability to trace any finished component back to the specific raw material lots, production date, operator, and equipment used.
  • First Article Inspection Report (FAIR): A comprehensive dimensional and electrical inspection of the first production unit, documented per AS9102 format.

This documentation is retained for a minimum period (often seven to ten years, sometimes longer for nuclear or submarine applications) and must be retrievable on request.

Reliability Testing and Screening

Military components undergo qualification testing and, in many cases, screening of 100% of production units to weed out infant mortality failures before they reach the field.

Qualification Testing

Qualification tests are performed on a sample of components to verify the design meets all requirements. These tests typically include:

  • Temperature cycling (typically minus 55 to plus 125 degrees Celsius, hundreds of cycles)
  • Thermal shock (rapid transition between temperature extremes)
  • Vibration testing (sinusoidal and random profiles)
  • Mechanical shock testing
  • Humidity testing (85 degrees Celsius, 85% relative humidity for extended duration)
  • Salt spray exposure (for naval applications)
  • Altitude testing (for airborne applications)
  • Life testing (extended operation at rated conditions to verify long-term reliability)

Production Screening

After qualification, every production unit may undergo screening to detect latent defects. Common screening methods include burn-in (operating the component at elevated temperature and electrical stress for a defined period), temperature cycling (a reduced number of cycles compared to qualification), and 100% electrical testing with tighter limits than the specification requires. Components that pass screening have a statistically lower probability of early failure in the field.

Conformal Coating and Hermetic Sealing

For components exposed to moisture, corrosive atmospheres, or contamination, additional protection beyond the standard core coating is required.

Conformal Coating

A thin layer of protective material (acrylic, urethane, silicone, or epoxy) is applied over the finished component to seal it against environmental exposure. MIL-I-46058 defines the requirements for conformal coating materials used in military electronics. The coating is typically applied by dipping, spraying, or selective coating, and cured to form a continuous, adherent film.

Hermetic Sealing

For the most demanding environments (submarines, spacecraft, deeply embedded sensors), components may require hermetic sealing. The component is placed inside a metal or ceramic housing, and the housing is sealed by welding, soldering, or glass-to-metal seals. Hermeticity is verified by helium leak testing per MIL-STD-883, with leak rates typically required below 1 x 10-8 atm-cc/sec.

Defense Applications

Custom magnetic components serve critical functions across virtually every defense electronic system.

Radar Systems

Radar transmitters and receivers rely on precision transformers for pulse forming, impedance matching, and power conversion. The pulse transformers in radar modulators must handle high peak powers with extremely fast rise times, requiring careful control of leakage inductance and distributed capacitance.

Communications Equipment

Military radios, satellite communications terminals, and electronic warfare systems use inductors and transformers for frequency filtering, impedance matching, and power supply regulation. These components must maintain their parameters across wide temperature ranges while handling the vibration environments of vehicles, aircraft, and man-portable equipment.

Power Systems

Every military platform has a power distribution system that converts, regulates, and conditions electrical power. The inductors in DC-DC converters, the transformers in isolated power supplies, and the chokes in EMI filters are all custom wound to meet the specific voltage, current, and environmental requirements of the platform.

Avionics

Flight control systems, navigation equipment, and cockpit displays all require reliable power conditioning. The magnetic components in these systems must meet the vibration and temperature requirements of the airborne environment while maintaining the tight electrical tolerances that flight-critical systems demand.
