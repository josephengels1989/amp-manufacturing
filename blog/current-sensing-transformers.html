Current Sensing Transformers: How They Work and Why Accuracy Matters

Current sensing transformers are critical components in power monitoring, metering, and protection circuits. Their accuracy depends on careful design decisions in core material, winding, and circuit integration.

Operating Principle

A current sensing transformer (also called a current transformer, or CT) operates on the principle of electromagnetic induction. When alternating current flows through a primary conductor (which can be a single wire passing through the center of a toroidal core, or a single-turn winding), it creates a time-varying magnetic flux in the core. This flux, in turn, induces a proportional voltage in the secondary winding, which is wound around the toroidal core with many turns.

The fundamental relationship is defined by the turns ratio. If the primary has Np turns and the secondary has Ns turns, the secondary current Is is related to the primary current Ip by:

Is = Ip x (Np / Ns)

For a current transformer with a single primary turn (one wire passing through the core) and 1000 secondary turns, a primary current of 100 A produces a secondary current of 0.1 A (100 mA). This scaled-down current can be safely measured by monitoring electronics without direct connection to the high-current circuit.

Key Advantage: Galvanic Isolation

Because the primary and secondary circuits are coupled only through the magnetic field in the core, there is complete electrical isolation between them. This allows current sensing in high-voltage circuits without exposing the measurement electronics to dangerous potentials. The isolation voltage rating depends on the insulation system between the primary conductor and the secondary winding.

Core Material Selection for Accuracy

The core material is the single most important factor in determining the accuracy of a current sensing transformer. The ideal core material would have infinite permeability, zero hysteresis, and zero core loss. Real materials represent different tradeoffs along these axes.

High-Permeability Alloys (1J85 / Permalloy / 80% Nickel-Iron)

For the highest accuracy current sensing, 1J85 (also known as Permalloy or 80% nickel-iron alloy) is the premium choice. With initial permeability values exceeding 100,000, this material minimizes the magnetizing current that the transformer "steals" from the measurement signal. Lower magnetizing current means the secondary current more accurately reflects the true primary current.

1J85 cores are typically tape-wound into toroidal shapes and coated with epoxy for electrical isolation. They excel in precision metering applications where accuracy classes of 0.1% or better are required.

Permeability and Accuracy

Higher core permeability directly translates to lower phase error and better ratio accuracy. A core with permeability of 100,000 will have roughly 10x less magnetizing current error than a core with permeability of 10,000 at the same operating point. For revenue metering and protection applications, this difference matters.

Grain-Oriented Silicon-Iron (3% Si-Fe)

Grain-oriented 3% silicon-iron is the workhorse material for current sensing transformers in industrial applications. It offers a good balance of permeability (up to 30,000+ in tape-wound toroidal form), high saturation flux density (1.8 to 2.0 T), and reasonable cost. The high saturation point means the core can handle higher primary currents before saturating, making it suitable for current indicators and trip circuits.

Silicon-iron cores are available in various tape thicknesses. Thinner tape (7 mil, 9 mil) reduces eddy current losses for improved performance at higher frequencies or under transient conditions.

Ferrite

Ferrite cores are used in current sensing applications that operate at higher frequencies, such as switching power supply current monitoring or pulse current measurement. MnZn ferrites with initial permeability of 5,000 to 10,000 provide adequate accuracy for many industrial applications at frequencies from tens of kilohertz to several megahertz. Ferrite is also the most cost-effective option for applications where moderate accuracy is acceptable.

Winding Considerations

Turns Count and Ratio

The secondary turns count determines the current transformation ratio and directly affects both accuracy and impedance. Common turn counts for current sensing secondaries range from 200 turns for low-ratio designs to 1,500 turns or more for high-sensitivity, low-current applications.

Higher turns counts provide greater sensitivity (more output voltage per ampere of primary current) but increase secondary winding resistance, which introduces measurement error under load. The designer must balance sensitivity against winding resistance to achieve the target accuracy at the expected operating current range.

Winding Distribution

For current sensing transformers, even distribution of the secondary winding around the full 360 degrees of the toroidal core is essential. Concentrated or uneven winding creates localized regions of higher and lower flux density, which degrades accuracy and makes the transformer sensitive to the position and orientation of the primary conductor relative to the core.

In a well-manufactured current sensing transformer, the winding machine distributes turns uniformly in one or more complete laps around the core. For high-accuracy designs, three complete laps (each covering 360 degrees) provide better uniformity than a single layer that fills the window.

Wire Gauge Selection

The wire gauge for the secondary winding is chosen based on the expected secondary current and the available window area. For current transformers with high turns ratios (and therefore low secondary currents), fine wire (30 to 34 AWG) is common. The key constraint is that the total winding resistance must remain low enough to avoid excessive voltage drop across the winding, which would reduce the current available to drive the burden resistor.

Winding Resistance and Accuracy

The secondary winding resistance effectively adds to the burden resistance, changing the actual operating point of the transformer. If the secondary winding has 20 ohms of DCR and the external burden resistor is 100 ohms, the total burden is 120 ohms. Ignoring the winding resistance in the circuit design will introduce a systematic error in the current measurement.

The Burden Resistor

The burden resistor is the load connected across the secondary winding of the current transformer. It converts the secondary current to a voltage signal that can be measured by an ADC, comparator, or other monitoring circuit.

The burden resistance value is critical. Too high a burden impedance can drive the core into saturation at peak currents, causing severe waveform distortion and measurement error. Too low a burden wastes signal amplitude and may require more amplifier gain downstream.

Selecting the Burden Value

The burden resistor should be sized so that at the maximum primary current, the secondary voltage remains well below the saturation voltage of the transformer. A practical approach:

  1. Determine the maximum primary current (Ip_max)
  2. Calculate the secondary current: Is_max = Ip_max / N (where N is the turns ratio)
  3. Choose a burden resistance that produces a convenient output voltage: Vout = Is_max x R_burden
  4. Verify that the required flux density at this operating point is well below the core's saturation flux density

Error Sources in Current Transformers

Understanding error sources helps engineers specify the right components and achieve the target system accuracy.

Ratio Error

Ratio error is the deviation of the actual current ratio from the nominal turns ratio. It is caused primarily by the magnetizing current that flows through the core's magnetizing inductance and does not contribute to the secondary output. Higher core permeability reduces ratio error.

Phase Error

In an ideal current transformer, the secondary current is exactly 180 degrees out of phase with the primary current. In practice, the magnetizing current introduces a small phase displacement. For power measurement applications (where both voltage and current phase must be known accurately), phase error directly affects the accuracy of the power calculation. Phase errors are typically specified in minutes of arc (arcmin) or milliradians.

Saturation Error

When the core approaches magnetic saturation, the relationship between primary and secondary current becomes highly nonlinear. The output waveform clips, and the RMS measurement becomes inaccurate. Current transformers must be designed with sufficient headroom to handle the maximum expected current, including transient peaks and inrush events.

Temperature Effects

Both core permeability and winding resistance change with temperature. Copper winding resistance increases by approximately 0.4% per degree Celsius, which directly affects the burden calculation. Core permeability variation depends on the material: silicon-iron is relatively stable, while ferrite permeability can change significantly over the industrial temperature range.

Applications

Power Monitoring and Energy Metering

Current transformers are fundamental components in electrical metering systems. Revenue-grade meters require accuracy classes of 0.2% or better, demanding high-permeability core materials and precision winding. Sub-metering systems for building energy management typically use 0.5% to 1.0% accuracy transformers.

Overcurrent Protection

Protection relays and circuit breakers use current transformers to detect fault currents. For protection applications, the transformer must maintain reasonable accuracy up to many times its rated current (typically 20x rated current for 5P20 class protection CTs). High saturation flux density is essential here, favoring silicon-iron cores.

Motor Control and Variable Frequency Drives

VFDs use current feedback for motor torque control. The current sensing transformer must accurately measure both the fundamental and harmonic components of the motor current. Bandwidth and phase accuracy at harmonic frequencies are important design considerations.

Power Quality Analysis

Instruments that measure power quality parameters (THD, power factor, harmonic spectrum) require current transformers with wide bandwidth and flat frequency response. The core material and burden circuit must be designed to maintain accuracy across the measurement frequency range, typically from the fundamental (50/60 Hz) up to the 50th harmonic (2.5 to 3 kHz).

Ground Fault Detection

Zero-sequence current transformers (also called ground fault sensors) pass all phase conductors through a single core. Under normal balanced conditions, the net flux is zero. A ground fault creates an imbalance that the CT detects. High core permeability is critical for detecting the small imbalance currents associated with early-stage ground faults.

Safety Considerations

Open-Circuit Warning

A current transformer secondary must never be left open-circuited while primary current is flowing. With no burden to limit the secondary voltage, the entire magnetizing flux drives an increasingly high voltage across the open secondary terminals, potentially reaching thousands of volts. This is a serious safety hazard and can also damage the insulation and permanently magnetize the core. Always short-circuit the secondary before disconnecting the burden.
